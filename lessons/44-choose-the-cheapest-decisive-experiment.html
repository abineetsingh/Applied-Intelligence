Choose the cheapest decisive experiment

Match experiment strength to the assumption and the commitment it must justify.

Mission connection

Founder judgment creates asymmetry by finding costly, reachable problems and buying evidence before committing to a solution.

Retrieve before reading

Why are concrete past-behavior questions stronger than hypothetical interest questions?

Reveal after answering

They reveal actual actions, constraints, costs, and consequences rather than politeness or inaccurate forecasts of future behavior.

The mental model

An experiment is valuable when it can change a decision. Use the cheapest test that creates evidence strong enough for the next commitment—not the cheapest activity or the most realistic prototype by default.

  1. Select one critical assumption.
  2. Define pass, fail, and ambiguous thresholds.
  3. List experiments from weak to strong evidence.
  4. Choose minimum cost at adequate strength.
  5. Precommit the decision following each result.

Worked case: testing willingness to pay

Interviews can refine the problem, but a paid pilot, deposit, signed procurement step, or real checkout creates stronger pricing evidence. A polished free prototype may test usability while leaving willingness to pay untouched.

Deliberate practice

For one top assumption, design three tests at increasing cost and evidence strength. Select the cheapest that can justify the next irreversible commitment.

Deliverable: An experiment ladder with thresholds, cost, duration, and result-to-action mapping.
