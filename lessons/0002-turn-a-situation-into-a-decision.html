Lesson 2 of 67 · 15–20 minutes

Turn a situation into a decision

Replace a cloud of concern with one choice that has an owner and a clock.

Mission connection

Exceptional judgment starts by representing the right problem. A clean representation prevents speed from becoming fast confusion.

Retrieve before reading

Why can a bad outcome follow a sound decision? Answer in one sentence.

Reveal after answering

Outcomes include uncertainty outside the decision maker's control; process quality must be judged using information available when the choice was made.

The mental model

Problems become tractable when phrased as decisions. “Growth is slowing” is a situation; “Which customer segment should we prioritize for the next six weeks?” is a decision. A usable frame names the choice, decision maker, deadline, objective, and boundaries.

  1. Write We must choose whether/which…
  2. Name the owner; advice without ownership is not a decision.
  3. Set the decision deadline, distinct from the outcome date.
  4. State the objective and one important constraint.
  5. List what the frame deliberately excludes.

Worked case: a founder facing weak growth

“Our product is not growing” invites endless analysis. Reframe it as: “By Friday, should we spend the next six-week cycle improving activation for current design partners, prospecting a second segment, or pausing development to revalidate the problem?” The new frame exposes alternatives, a deadline, and an allocation decision.

Deliberate practice

Take one concern currently occupying your attention. Write three different decision frames around it: narrow, medium, and broad. Select the frame whose answer would most change your next action.

Deliverable: One sentence containing the choice, owner, deadline, objective, and scope.

Check your judgment

Answer before rereading. Feedback appears immediately.

Transfer it

Apply the same frame to a trade: not “Is the market bullish?” but “Given this horizon and risk limit, do I enter, wait, or reject this setup by the close?”
