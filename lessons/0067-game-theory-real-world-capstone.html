Capstone: change the game

Integrate equilibrium, commitment, repetition, information, and mechanism design in a live strategic decision.

Mission connection

Exceptional judgment anticipates adaptation. Game theory turns customers, competitors, partners, employees, and market participants from static assumptions into agents with incentives, information, and responses of their own.

Retrieve before reading

What determines bargaining power more reliably than forceful performance?

Reveal after answering

Credible outside options, relative patience, information, commitment, and each side's cost of delay determine leverage more reliably than aggressive presentation.

The mental model

The strongest game-theoretic move is often not selecting a clever strategy inside the current game. It is changing participation, information, timing, payoffs, commitments, or enforcement so the desirable behavior becomes individually rational and stable.

  1. Model the current game and at least one rival representation.
  2. Find best responses, equilibria, and unstable assumptions.
  3. Solve sequential branches and test credibility.
  4. Add repetition, information asymmetry, and bounded rationality.
  5. Redesign one rule and predict adaptation before acting.

Worked case: a platform faces a subsidy war

Two platforms subsidize users while suppliers multi-home. Matching the rival may be a dominated race toward weak unit economics; exiting may surrender network density. The operator compares regional focus, exclusive supply contracts, interoperability, loyalty benefits, and a differentiated segment. Each move changes rivals' and participants' incentives, so the strategy must include their likely adaptation and a stop rule.

Deliberate practice

Produce a one-page game brief: players, strategies, payoffs, information, timing, equilibrium, credible commitments, repeated-game effects, signals, screens, and one rule change. Red-team it from another player's perspective.
