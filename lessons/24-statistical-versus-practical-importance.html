Statistical versus practical importance

A tiny effect can be certain and useless; a valuable effect can remain uncertain.

Mission connection

Exceptional judgment distinguishes a noisy estimate from evidence strong enough to change a costly action.

Retrieve before reading

State the p-value inversion error in symbols or words.

Reveal after answering

P(data | null) is not P(null | data); a p-value does not directly give the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

The mental model

Statistical significance depends on effect, noise, sample size, and procedure. Practical importance depends on value, costs, risk, and alternatives. Large samples can detect trivial effects; small samples can leave valuable effects unresolved.

  1. Define the action threshold before looking at significance.
  2. Translate effect size into money, time, risk, or user outcome.
  3. Include implementation and opportunity costs.
  4. Inspect uncertainty around net value.
  5. Choose whether to act, test, or ignore.

Worked case: a measurable latency improvement

A 5-millisecond improvement may be statistically decisive across billions of requests yet irrelevant to users—or highly valuable if it crosses a real-time bidding deadline. Context converts effect size into utility.

Deliberate practice

Take one significant or impressive metric. Convert its effect into a unit the decision values and subtract implementation cost. Evaluate low, central, and high estimates.
