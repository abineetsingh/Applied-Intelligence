Rebuild from primitives

Turn parts into competing mechanisms instead of a single persuasive story.

Mission connection

Exceptional judgment starts by representing the right problem. A clean representation prevents speed from becoming fast confusion.

Retrieve before reading

Name the four tests that tell you an element is atomic enough for decision work.

Reveal after answering

It can be observed, estimated, changed, or treated as a genuine hard constraint—and further detail would not alter the choice.

The mental model

Decomposition creates parts; modeling proposes relationships. Build upward by connecting causes to effects, then force at least one competing model. A model earns attention by making different predictions, not by sounding coherent.

  1. Connect primitives with explicit because statements.
  2. Write the mechanism as if → then → because.
  3. Construct a rival explanation using the same observations.
  4. Name one observation on which the models disagree.
  5. Prefer the next test that best separates them.

Worked case: a sudden retention decline

Model A says a new onboarding step blocks activation; it predicts the decline is concentrated among new accounts exposed to the step. Model B says the acquired cohort changed; it predicts lower retention even among users who complete onboarding. The same aggregate chart fits both stories, but a cohort split distinguishes them.

Deliberate practice

Take one belief you currently hold strongly. Write its mechanism in three arrows, then create a rival mechanism that explains the same observations. Identify the cheapest discriminating observation.

Deliverable: Two causal chains and one discriminating test.

Check your judgment

Answer before rereading. Feedback appears immediately.

Transfer it

When assessing a market move, compare at least two mechanisms—new information versus liquidity-driven flow—and ask what each predicts beyond the price move itself.
